For one week, sometimes two, in late June or early July, the beaches along Newfoundland's east coast fill with people carrying buckets, dip nets, and pillowcases. They walk down to the water at high tide, often after dark, and stand at the edge of the surf. Within minutes, the surf is no longer water. It is fish — millions of them, packed shoulder to shoulder, riding the incoming waves and beaching themselves on the sand to spawn.

This is the capelin roll. It is the most reliable mass-spawning event in the Newfoundland calendar, the moment when the entire offshore food web compresses itself onto a strip of beach a few metres wide, and the moment when the rest of the province — cod, whales, seabirds, and people — comes to collect.


Almost everything on this coast connects, in one way or another, to what those fish do for that one week. That is the simplest description of what follows.


## What the Fish Is


![image.jpeg](https://prod-files-secure.s3.us-west-2.amazonaws.com/1b2d553c-979e-4b24-9706-a629fdf32213/1844c75f-56b7-4201-b8f9-648986553863/image.jpeg?X-Amz-Algorithm=AWS4-HMAC-SHA256&X-Amz-Content-Sha256=UNSIGNED-PAYLOAD&X-Amz-Credential=ASIAZI2LB466Z2UDVW5M%2F20260504%2Fus-west-2%2Fs3%2Faws4_request&X-Amz-Date=20260504T204724Z&X-Amz-Expires=3600&X-Amz-Security-Token=IQoJb3JpZ2luX2VjEKz%2F%2F%2F%2F%2F%2F%2F%2F%2F%2FwEaCXVzLXdlc3QtMiJIMEYCIQCkQxAUyGISE0RJP4JQ8r3Luv3GGjyPIJQANSz%2FXvgJnwIhANzf262nh281sn6ZpRtoWMUr6rb%2F7H%2FjpANjZmP0WRGgKv8DCHQQABoMNjM3NDIzMTgzODA1IgyilUJpTebb6hM%2B%2BEEq3APeWlTKZbe0%2FYXHCN3c28aS%2FoiEgQuYncdxDzxWyyCi5AZr4xV7%2BlcEJhTX02zr%2F6aFJVfjgp5dCqGvYLit%2F9kpuwWZegmJURvMnrymvT7hGcdZNb4E6RxMiGwX59xF3gAUbSzEkMsdT3DNEGfba2VBU9rPCr7geoR2epk59BJj%2BcDly%2B0y974f7gqX6YN0aYr5EYB0hm6%2FN2oSSemr%2BS2Jziwk9b6Gw3rvAQO1V6yL9rDSW7ocMviKnmq%2BGyJwDSq%2BM9oQAXayvULodsHRoGz09phEFTi0P3B7c0hEbAdJKWUA5lBhS%2FKtIQW4qmpH7xkuOjH4uCRgQLviYywHhhIyVAIXc9YAew2slU7TaLr0lW3BUqulqnrR8iTPrFyp3LSOBp5RNBuzLZkfhMmd2JHJuhN98uDfnDwL6bNsylL1gOHMmEpwQZ0beyMDzOZULWAphu0GolD2ZJFu1QwhfX2l8rkagFwlueS7xfj5UDPCawzevXUhDQqyrYB9VFcO76UrzLvflAS5vqhgkUqM%2BYyN8XLNB%2FFLN43BVrsXsPhmvVjgObj9wh1mf%2BQnp3VX%2FOzalNnuPzQ53JWSkG3rhTqqaQoCNYFrKeUDQg%2F4%2FwdsRQS7nghWJpSpSOJvjjD75OPPBjqkAYR5N3NcJHxcEjJyQQJkitDlrnKFeIu6G3bcWJxAj5P6CeggdX5UxPjxhIDsoPb%2FkO%2FNLgd3LpI4rUclBqp0kK%2BVZTPqGoLKWku26Osugm6dPEKRb28u3vCGqnmMZ9RmaecUj384pcKbF80nGtmS02dKKPOgtT3%2FKR0a%2FbZUCtMfeSoyusazLWHmPoWTnnD2s78RfZqX0NXNnymGbcLLaf%2BGztGi&X-Amz-Signature=15f846adccd13192a543f8e6c50d74d330af53377aea7eccb1b879f60c8d928c&X-Amz-SignedHeaders=host&x-amz-checksum-mode=ENABLED&x-id=GetObject)


Capelin (_Mallotus villosus_) are small smelt-family fish, 15–20 cm long, olive on the back and silver on the sides. Hold one up and it does not look like much. There are several reasons it is one of the most important fish in the North Atlantic.


The first is volume. Capelin live three to five years, grow fast, reproduce in extraordinary numbers, and die at high rates after spawning. A single female releases six to twelve thousand eggs in a season. Multiplied across a population that can run into the tens of billions, the result is a wall of biomass that nothing else in the ecosystem can match.


The second is position. Capelin sit in the middle of the food web — they eat plankton, and almost everything bigger eats them. Cod. Humpback whales. Harp seals. Squid. Puffins, murres, gannets, kittiwakes. The list is long, and the dependency is one-way. The predators that define this coast either follow capelin during the spawning season or have life cycles tuned to capelin abundance. Without the small silver fish in the middle, the apex animals on top do not have a stable base.


The third is the way capelin connect things that would otherwise be separate. The cold offshore current and the warm inshore beach. The plankton bloom and the cod fishery. The arriving icebergs and the spawning surf. The science of the fish and the household memory of a generation that grew up scooping it out of the waves. Every layer of the system passes through capelin at some point in the year.


That is what makes them worth a long look.


## The Cold-Water System: What Capelin and Icebergs Share


The reason capelin can spawn here at all is the same reason icebergs reach this coast.

The Labrador Current carries Arctic and Labrador-Sea water southward along Newfoundland's east coast, holding annual sea temperatures roughly 7–10 °C below what the latitude would otherwise produce. Without it, the inshore water in summer would be too warm for capelin spawning, which has a fairly narrow optimal range of 6–10 °C. The current is what keeps the door open.


The same current carries icebergs of Greenlandic origin south from spring through early summer, producing the stretch of coast that tourism boards now call Iceberg Alley. The iceberg season (April–July) and the capelin spawning season (June–August) overlap in time and place. It is tempting to read this overlap as causal — that the icebergs are part of why the capelin can spawn — but the evidence for that link is thinner than the postcards suggest. Iceberg melt produces local cooling, water-column mixing, and nutrient input, but the effects are spatially limited and no direct contribution to capelin recruitment has been demonstrated.


A cautious version of the story is more useful. The Labrador Current does the heavy lifting on temperature. Icebergs may make a marginal contribution by holding water locally cool in late capelin seasons, when surface warming might otherwise push spawning beaches above the 10 °C ceiling. The qualifier matters. Late-spawning capelin, even when they manage to spawn, often produce offspring that fail to grow large enough to survive their first winter. Whatever icebergs are doing for the system, they are not rescuing the late seasons.


The same cold water that brings the icebergs brings the fish. The same cold water that closes the harbours in spring is what holds the spawning window open in summer. The two things that most define the look of this coast in the warmer months — the white shapes drifting past and the silver wall on the beach — are produced by the same current. Everything is connected.


## The Migration Inshore


In late spring, mature capelin begin a coordinated migration from the offshore feeding grounds to the inshore spawning beaches — a journey of more than 350 km in some cases.


The migration is not random. It is staged by size, age, and reproductive status. Larger, older, previously-spawned fish arrive at the beaches first; smaller and first-time spawners follow weeks later. Water temperature, often assumed to be the cue that triggers the migration, appears instead to function as an indirect regulator: it sets the speed of growth and gonadal maturation, which in turn determine when each fish is ready to spawn. In cold years, the whole migration can be delayed by weeks.


As the schools approach the coast, they aggregate in the major bays — Trinity, Notre Dame, Conception — and the sexes separate. Males arrive first and stage near the spawning beaches. Females, still maturing their eggs, hold offshore until later. This is one of the patterns that local fishers know about long before the literature confirms it. Anyone who fishes for cod or squid that follow the capelin in learns the male-then-female sequence by watching what is in the stomachs of the predators. The ecology is also a kind of folk knowledge, transmitted through cleaning fish on the wharf.


The two ways of knowing — formal science and inherited fishing practice — describe the same thing in different vocabularies. They are connected, too.


## Spawning: The Roll Itself


Spawning happens on two kinds of substrate: sand and fine gravel beaches, and the sea bottom in 5–40 m of water. Beach spawning concentrates at night, on a rising tide, in water of 6–10 °C. Males use a specialized sensory organ called the spawning carina along their flank to align with females and assist the spawning act. The eggs are sticky and fix to the substrate on contact.


A female releases six to twelve thousand eggs and then withdraws back into deeper water. Males stay on the beach, mating repeatedly, and die in large numbers — partly from physical exhaustion, partly from stranding as the tide recedes, partly from predators that have learned to wait at the same beach for the same week every year. The asymmetry has a clean evolutionary description: in capelin, males effectively breed once and die, while females survive to spawn again across multiple seasons. The roll is a male sacrifice as much as it is a female delivery.


The two spawning modes — beach and bottom — are not separate populations. The same fish will choose one or the other based on the conditions of the year. In warm years, more spawning happens on the bottom; in cool years, the beach roll dominates. The choice is opportunistic, made on a season-by-season basis rather than written into separate genetic lines.


Eggs hatch in roughly two weeks. The larvae drift in the inshore plankton, and their survival depends on whether the timing of the hatch matches the seasonal peak of plankton production. If the plankton blooms early and the larvae arrive late — or the other way around — most of them starve. Onshore winds, which transport the larvae from the spawning beaches into productive nearshore water, are one of the major physical determinants of how many of the next generation will live. The spawning event is not the end of the bottleneck. The first weeks of larval life are a second filter, harder to see and at least as decisive.


The whole sequence is a stack of windows that have to align. Temperature window. Tide window. Wind window. Plankton window. The roll on the beach is what the system looks like when, for one week, the windows line up.


## Why Newfoundland Cares


The cod fishery that built Newfoundland was, indirectly, a capelin fishery. Cod follow capelin inshore, gorge on them, and that is when and where they were historically caught. For four hundred years, the working calendar of the outport — when men went on the water, when stages were repaired, when fish were split and salted — was set, in the end, by what the capelin were doing.


The non-commercial relationship is older and stranger than that, and it ran in parallel.


Long before capelin became a commercial product (mostly export to Japan for roe, beginning in the 1970s), Newfoundlanders harvested them by the bucket from the beach during the roll. They dried them on flake, used them to fertilize potato gardens, fed them to dogs, and ate them themselves. In some communities, families kept their own stretch of beach as a known capelin spot, the way other places might keep a known berry patch. The fish was not bought. It was scooped.


This is the part of the relationship that has lasted longest. Almost no one in Newfoundland alive today has worked the cod fishery in its industrial form — that ended with the moratorium in 1992. But many people still go down to the beach in late June and fill a bucket. The roll is one of the few elements of the original cod-era food economy that survived intact into the twenty-first century. It survived because it never depended on a market in the first place.


It is worth pausing on what kind of cultural inheritance this is. The roll is not a festival. There is no organizing committee, no parade, no permits, no entrance fee. It happens on a tide that the moon decides. People show up because their parents showed up, who showed up because their parents showed up. The transmission is almost entirely informal — passed from one generation to the next in the form of phone calls ("they're in at Middle Cove tonight"), front-page photos in _The Telegram_, kids being handed a bucket and told to put their boots on. It is one of the most reliable traditions in the province, and it has no central institution behind it at all.


Compare it to mummering, which had to be banned for over a century, then revived through a song and a folklore student and a heritage office. Capelin needed none of that. The roll preserved itself, because the conditions that produced it were not cultural. They were ecological. As long as the fish came back every June, so did the people. The custom and the species were the same custom.


That is also, of course, what makes the recent decades so worrying.


## The Collapse


In 1991, the year before the cod moratorium, the capelin stock collapsed. It has not recovered. For the past three decades, biomass has stayed at a fraction of pre-1991 levels, and the population has remained in a state of chronic low productivity.


The contributing factors stack on top of each other. Spawning timing has slipped later in the year. The onshore winds that move larvae into productive water have weakened. Sea temperatures have shifted in ways that decouple capelin from their plankton prey. The seal population, freed by the collapse of the commercial seal hunt, has grown. No single factor explains the collapse. They reinforce each other.


The downstream consequences are familiar to anyone watching the cod recovery effort. Cod cannot rebuild without capelin to eat, and the capelin are not coming back on a schedule that the cod can wait for. Bird colonies — Witless Bay, Cape St. Mary's — have shown recurring breeding failures linked to capelin shortages. Whale-watching operators report changes in humpback distribution that track capelin distribution. The collapse of one mid-trophic species has propagated upward through the food web in ways the historical fishery science was not designed to predict.


The cultural consequences are quieter and harder to measure, but they are real. The roll is still happening, but it is happening less reliably. It used to be a mid-June event you could roughly count on. In recent decades it has slipped later, sometimes well into July, and individual years have produced no roll at all on beaches that historically had one every season. Children who would once have grown up scooping fish off the sand have grown up not doing so, because in their year there were no fish to scoop.


A custom that survived industrial fishing, the cod collapse, the emptying of the outports, and the digital age is now being thinned out, slowly, by the disappearance of the species itself. The thread that ran through everything is fraying in the middle.


## The Seal Question


The most contested part of the story is the role of harp seals.


The argument from one side is straightforward. Seals eat capelin. The seal population has grown substantially since the dramatic reductions of the commercial hunt in the 1970s and 1980s. Therefore seals are a major reason the capelin stock has not recovered, and the recovery of cod and capelin will require some kind of culling or expanded commercial harvest. This is the position taken by parts of the inshore fishery and by some politicians.


The argument from the other side is also straightforward. There is no clean evidence that seals are the limiting factor on capelin recovery. Seals eat many things. The collapse predates the seal population peak. The rest of the system — temperature, winds, plankton timing — has changed in ways that would suppress capelin even with no seals at all. Calling seals the obstacle to recovery is, on this view, a politically convenient simplification of a more complicated picture.


A careful reading sits closer to the second view, but with caveats. Seals do consume capelin, and the population is large. The question is not whether they eat fish, but whether their predation is enough, on its own, to hold a recovery down. The available evidence does not unambiguously support that claim. A more accurate description is probably this: the decline of large predatory fish, the loss of cod, and the collapse of capelin together produced an altered system in which seals are now a more visible component than they used to be — but identifying them as the cause of the failure to recover is harder than the bumper-sticker version suggests.


The trade in seal products is a separate layer again. Most countries have banned the import of commercial seal products, and the bans were defended primarily on animal-welfare grounds, not ecological ones. Most observers — including most working scientists — agree that those bans were not adopted on the basis of ecosystem science. Whether they should be relaxed for ecosystem reasons is a different question, and one without a clean answer. It is possible to think the bans were not scientifically grounded _and_ to think that lifting them would not, in fact, do much to help capelin or cod. Both of those things can be true at once.


This is the kind of debate where it is easy to take a position quickly and harder to take one carefully.


## What to Watch on a Capelin Beach


For anyone who wants to see the roll, the basics are simple. The roll happens somewhere along the east coast every June or July. Conception Bay, Trinity Bay, and Notre Dame Bay are the main areas. The roll is most active on a rising tide after dark, in calm weather, when the water along the beach sits between roughly 6 and 10 °C. Local CBC and VOCM coverage will name specific beaches once the fish start showing up. So will Twitter, faster.


Bring a bucket. A dip net helps but is not necessary; the fish will roll right up onto the sand. Newfoundland regulations permit recreational harvest by hand and dip net within set limits — check the current DFO bulletin before going.


What to actually watch for, beyond the obvious:


The predators that arrive with the capelin. Humpback whales lunge-feeding metres offshore. Gulls and gannets working the surface. Cod sometimes visible in shallow water, doing what made them the basis of an entire colony's economy for four hundred years. The capelin roll is the only event of the year that pulls the entire vertical food web — plankton, fish, birds, mammals, humans — onto a single twenty-metre stretch of sand for a few hours.


The timing. The roll used to be a reliable mid-June event. The fact that it now slips later in some years, and fails to materialize in others, is not just an inconvenience for the people with buckets. It is a leading indicator of what is happening to the rest of the system.


The continuity. Whoever is on the beach with you that night is likely to be standing within a few kilometres of where their grandparents once stood, doing the same thing, for reasons that have not really changed. The fish are the same. The tide is the same. The buckets are mostly the same. Capelin nights are one of the few experiences in a fast-changing province that still connect a person directly to the way an outport summer used to feel.


## Why It Matters


Capelin are easy to underestimate. They are small, they live short lives, they exist in unimaginable numbers. The instinct is to treat them as background biology — the food layer, the thing the more interesting animals eat.


But almost everything that defines the marine identity of this coast — the cod that built the colony, the whales that bring tourists, the seabirds that fill the cliffs, the icebergs that pose for photographs, the families that still go down to the beach with buckets — depends, directly or indirectly, on capelin doing what they do every June. The connections run through the middle of the system, where they are easy to miss. A long, slow collapse in the middle of a food web does not announce itself the way the collapse of a top predator does. It happens quietly, over decades, and by the time the consequences are obvious in the apex species, the middle layer has been hollowed out for a long time.


The capelin roll is one of the last places on this coast where the whole vertical structure — water, plankton, fish, birds, mammals, people — is visible at the same time and in the same place. Cold current. Warm beach. Spawning fish. Lunging whales. Children with buckets. The mid-trophic species is the connection between all of them.


Lose the middle and the layers stop talking to each other. That is, in the end, what it means to lose a keystone species. Not just that the species is gone, but that the system it held together comes apart in ways that do not reverse easily. The roll is still happening. Many of the layers are still here. The thing to watch is whether they stay connected.
